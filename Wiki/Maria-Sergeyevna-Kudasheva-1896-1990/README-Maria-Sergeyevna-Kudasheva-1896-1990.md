# Princess Maria (Myra) Sergeyevna Kudasheva Armour

**Princess Maria "Myra" Sergeyevna Kudasheva** (later **Myra Koudacheff Armour**; Russian: Княжна Мария Сергеевна Кудашева; 7 April 1895 or 1896 – 15 September 1990) was a Russian-born aristocrat and American diplomatic hostess. The daughter of Imperial Russian courtier Prince Sergei Vladimirovich Kudashev and Countess Vera Maximilianovna von Nieroth, she escaped Petrograd following the 1917 Russian Revolution with the assistance of American diplomat Norman Armour, whom she married in February 1919.[1][2][3] Over a diplomatic career spanning more than three decades, she accompanied her husband to major United States postings in Europe, North America, and South America—including Ottawa, Buenos Aires, Santiago, Madrid, and Caracas—and later donated his diplomatic papers to Princeton University.[3][4][5]

## Early life in St. Petersburg

Maria Sergeyevna Kudasheva—known throughout her life by the diminutive **Myra**—was born in St. Petersburg into the upper tier of the Russian Imperial aristocracy.[1][2] Her father, Prince Sergei Vladimirovich Kudashev (1863–1933), held the court ranks of *Kammerjunker* and *Kammerherr* (Chamberlain) under Emperor Nicholas II, served in the Main Directorate of Appanages, and sat on the boards of leading Russian financial and industrial enterprises.[1][6] Her mother, Countess Vera Maximilianovna von Nieroth (1874–1920), had served as a Maid of Honor (*freilina*) to Empress Alexandra Feodorovna and was the daughter of Imperial Master of the Hunt Count Maximilian von Nieroth and Anastasia Fyodorovna Trepova (sister of Prime Minister Alexander Trepov and Palace Commandant Dmitri Trepov).[1][7]

She grew up in St. Petersburg alongside her younger brother, Prince Sergei Sergeyevich Kudashev (1901–1991), residing at Mokhovaya Ulitsa, 6 and later at Bol'shaya Morskaya Ulitsa, 57—directly across the Moika Canal from the Yusupov Palace.[1][6][8] Raised in the multilingual environment of the St. Petersburg court nobility, she spoke fluent Russian, French, and English, and in 1915 met Norman Armour (1887–1982), a young American attaché then serving in Paris before his 1916 posting to the United States Embassy in Petrograd.[3][9]

## 1917 Revolution and escape from Russia

Following the February and October 1917 revolutions and the Bolshevik nationalization of private property, the Kudashev family's court titles and corporate positions placed them in acute danger.[6] With the assistance of Norman Armour—then Second Secretary at the American Embassy in Petrograd, who helped secure travel and identity papers for the family—Princess Maria fled Petrograd alongside her parents and brother across the Finnish border into neutral Sweden.[2][3][9]

In Sweden, the family settled at Villa Skärtofta in Saltsjöbaden, outside Stockholm, which became an informal gathering center for displaced Russian nobles and Allied diplomats.[2] Armour, who remained in Russia until August 1918 (staying behind at the relocated Embassy in Vologda before escaping through Moscow and Finland in September 1918), reunited with Princess Maria in Stockholm that autumn.[2] Their engagement was announced in *The New York Times* on 2 November 1918.[10]

## Marriage and diplomatic career

On 2 February 1919, Princess Maria married Norman Armour in an Orthodox ceremony at the Russian church in Stockholm, with United States Minister to Sweden Ira Nelson Morris serving as best man (contemporary press notices also noted Armour's new diplomatic assignment to the American Embassy in Brussels).[2][11] Their son, Norman Armour Jr., was born in Brussels in February 1920, shortly after Princess Vera Kudasheva's death in Paris in January 1920.[1][2]

Over the next three decades, Myra Armour accompanied her husband across a series of senior United States diplomatic postings, gaining a reputation in diplomatic memoirs and press profiles as an accomplished linguist and hostess:[4][5][12]

* **Europe and Washington (1919–1935):** Following assignments at the American legations and embassies in Brussels (1919–1920), The Hague (1920–1921), Montevideo (1921–1922), the Department of State in Washington, D.C. (1922–1924), Rome (1924–1925), Tokyo (1925–1928), and Paris (1928–1932), she served as hostess of the U.S. Legation in Port-au-Prince, Haiti, when Armour was appointed Minister to Haiti (1932–1935).[2][4]
* **Canada and Latin America (1935–1944):** She served as diplomatic hostess in Ottawa during Armour's tenure as U.S. Minister to Canada (1935–1938), where she sat for portrait photographer Yousuf Karsh,[13] and subsequently in Santiago as Ambassadress to Chile (1938–1939) and in Buenos Aires as Ambassadress to Argentina (1939–1944).[4][14] During World War II in Argentina, she was active in Allied relief and women's anti-fascist charitable initiatives alongside Argentine civic organizations.[14]
* **Spain, Washington, and Venezuela (1944–1955):** In December 1944, President Franklin D. Roosevelt appointed Norman Armour Ambassador to Spain (1944–1945), followed by service as Assistant Secretary of State for Political Affairs (1947–1948), Ambassador to Venezuela (1950–1951), and Ambassador to Guatemala (1954–1955).[4][5]

## Later life and legacy

Following Norman Armour's retirement from the Foreign Service, the couple divided their time between New York City, New Jersey, and summer residences in New England.[3][12] After her husband's death in September 1982 at age 94, Myra Armour preserved his historical records and in 1984 donated the **Norman Armour Papers (1913–1983)**—including his eyewitness recollections and Russian travel documents from the 1917 Revolution—to the Seeley G. Mudd Manuscript Library at Princeton University.[3]

She died on 15 September 1990 in the United States, surviving her husband by eight years.[1][15]

## Historiographical note

Biographical databases occasionally confuse Princess Maria (Myra) Sergeyevna Kudasheva Armour with her contemporary and distant kinswoman **Princess Maria Pavlovna Kudasheva** (née Cuvillier, 1895–1985), the Franco-Russian writer who married French author Romain Rolland in 1934.[1][2] Similarly, genealogical records vary between 7 April 1895 (cited in Jacques Ferrand's *Les familles princières de l'ancien Empire de Russie*) and 1896 for her birth year.[1][2]
