# James Cameron Marshall Shirk

**James C. Marshall Shirk** (23 June 1865 – 24 August 1918), known professionally as J. C. Marshall Shirk, was a Philadelphia architect. He trained in the office of Addison Hutton, studied at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, and practised on his own account in Philadelphia from 1893 until his death; he was elected to the American Institute of Architects in 1901.[1][2] His middle name is given as Cameron in some family records and as Carson in most other sources (see Open questions). He was the son of the naval officer [James Whitehill Shirk](https://theo-armour.github.io/genealogy/#Wiki/James-Whitehill-Shirk-1832-1873/README-James-Whitehill-Shirk-1832-1873.md), the father of [Anne Bell Burrage](https://theo-armour.github.io/genealogy/#Wiki/Anne-Bell-Burrage-1890-1983/README-Anne-Bell-Burrage-1890-1983.md), and Theo Armour's maternal great-grandfather.[1][8][12]

## Family and early life

He was born in Philadelphia on 23 June 1865, "the son of Commander James W. Shirk, U. S. N.", as the obituary in the *Journal of the American Institute of Architects* put it.[1] His father had commanded Union gunboats on the western rivers in the Civil War and was at the Philadelphia Navy Yard after it.[13] His mother, Mary W. Marshall, was a daughter of James C. Marshall of Erie, Pennsylvania, a lawyer who was president of the Marine National Bank of Erie from 1867.[6]

His father died in Washington in February 1873, when he was seven.[13] In 1884 the *History of Erie County* said of his mother that "she has three children, all living in Philadelphia".[6] A MyHeritage index entry for the 1880 census, quoted in one family tree, lists "J. Marshall Shirk" in Philadelphia with his parents' names and two siblings, David Marshall Shirk and Fannie Shirk; the census page has not been seen.[10]

## Training

He graduated from the Central High School of Philadelphia, in 1886 according to the Philadelphia Architects and Buildings biography by Sandra L. Tatman, and then entered the office of the architect Addison Hutton.[1][2] He stayed with Hutton until September 1888, "when he left for a 14-month sojourn in Europe", and studied at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris in the Atelier Pascal.[2] Tatman counts him among the first generation of Philadelphia architects to study at the École.[2]

## Practice

On his return to Philadelphia in 1891 he and Charles L. Hillman, another of Hutton's former pupils, set up the firm of Hillman & Shirk.[1][2] It lasted only a year under that name: in 1892 the two joined with Hutton.[1][2] In June 1893 Shirk withdrew and established an independent practice, with offices in the Philadelphia National Bank Building, "which continued until his death".[1][2] He joined the T-Square Club in 1891 and was elected to the American Institute of Architects in 1901.[1][2]

His obituary in the Institute's journal names three works: "the Pennsylvania State Hospital at Fairview, Pa; the Philadelphia Home for Incurables in that city; the Marine National Bank at Erie, Pa., as well as other minor public buildings and residences in and about Philadelphia".[1] Tatman describes the practice as mostly residential, with institutional commissions that included the Pennsylvania State Hospital for the Criminally Insane and several buildings for the Philadelphia Home for Incurables. She cites a letter he wrote on 30 September 1898 in which he listed a first prize for the War Library and Museum for the Loyal Legion, a first prize for a "building in Erie, Pa." (which she takes to be probably the Marine National Bank) and a third prize for the Pennsylvania State Building for the World's Fair.[2] The bank at Erie was the one of which his grandfather Marshall had been president.[6]

The Philadelphia Architects and Buildings database links two projects to him: the Children's House of the Home for Incurables, on South 49th Street near Woodland Avenue, Philadelphia, and a garage and stable for D. L. Ward at Bala Cynwyd. It also lists 74 published references not yet linked to a project, which have not been examined.[3]

## Marriage and family

He married Ida Watkins of Little Rock, Arkansas. One family tree gives the marriage at Philadelphia on 20 April 1892, citing Pennsylvania church records and the Philadelphia marriage index, neither of which has been seen.[11] A history of Christ Church parish in Little Rock, published in 1900, lists Ida among the children of Chief Justice George C. Watkins and Sophia Fulton, and says: "Ida married Marshall Shirk; they have one daughter, Anne Bell Shirk."[7] One tree gives Ida's birth as 13 February 1864 at Little Rock and her death as 21 November 1951 at Ipswich, Massachusetts.[11]

Their daughter, Anne Bell Shirk, was born in Philadelphia on 6 October in 1893 or 1890. In April 1915 she married Albert Cameron Burrage Jr of Boston; a Little Rock newspaper gave the bride's address as 1339 Pine Street, Philadelphia. She became a founder and the first president of The Herb Society of America.[8]

## Death

He died on Saturday, 24 August 1918.[1][4] The Philadelphia *Evening Public Ledger* of Monday, 26 August, under the heading "Bury J. C. Marshall Shirk", reported that the funeral of "J. C. Marshall Shirk, architect" was held that afternoon at 3 o'clock at 1820 Chestnut Street, and that he "died Saturday at his home" on South Rittenhouse Square; it added that his father was Commander James W. Shirk.[4] The Institute's journal and the *American Art Annual* both say that he died at Scranton, Pennsylvania.[1][5] Tatman notes that Mrs Shirk's letter informing the Institute of his death was written on the letterhead of the Hotel Miami in Dayton, Ohio.[2]

His place of burial has not been found. The Erie Cemetery records that list his parents have no entry for him.[9]

## Residences

* **Philadelphia.** Born there in 1865; listed there in the 1880 census index, and his mother's three children were "all living in Philadelphia" in 1884.[1][6][10]
* **Paris.** At the École des Beaux-Arts in the fourteen months after September 1888.[2]
* **Philadelphia.** Office in the Philadelphia National Bank Building from 1893.[2] His daughter's address in 1915 was 1339 Pine Street.[8] The newspaper report of his death gives his home as a number on South Rittenhouse Square that reads "1839" in the uncorrected OCR text.[4]
* **Scranton, Pennsylvania.** Where he died, according to the two professional obituaries.[1][5]

## Open questions

* **Middle name: Cameron or Carson.** The folder name, Geni and earlier versions of the profile page give **Cameron**.[8][10][12] The Philadelphia Architects and Buildings biography and four family-tree exports give **Carson**.[2][10][11] Two of those trees name his mother's father as "James Carson Marshall"; the 1884 county history calls the grandfather only "James C. Marshall".[6][10][11] Every record of his own time seen for this biography (the Institute's journal, the *American Art Annual*, the *Evening Public Ledger*, the Little Rock parish history) calls him J. C. Marshall Shirk or Marshall Shirk.[1][4][5][7] No record that spells out the middle name has been seen. Carson, as a grandfather's name, is the better supported; Cameron is also the middle name of his daughter's husband and father-in-law, which may be how it arose.
* **Place of death.** Scranton (the Institute's journal and the *American Art Annual*) or his home in Philadelphia (the *Evening Public Ledger*, and the family trees).[1][4][5][10][11] The funeral was in Philadelphia two days later.[4] Why he would have been at Scranton is not known.
* **Year of birth.** 23 June 1865 in the Institute's journal, the Philadelphia Architects and Buildings biography, Geni and three trees.[1][2][10][11] The OCR text of the *Evening Public Ledger* reads "1866", and the 1880 census index entry also gives 1866; the newspaper page image has not been checked.[4][10]
* **Which hospital, and where.** The Institute's journal says "the Pennsylvania State Hospital at Fairview, Pa".[1] The *American Art Annual* says "the Pennsylvania State Hospital and the Philadelphia Home for Incurables at Fairview, Pa."[5] Tatman places the State Hospital for the Criminally Insane at Harrisburg and the Home for Incurables at "Fairview".[2] The database's own project entry puts the Home for Incurables in Philadelphia.[3] These cannot all be right; the hospital's location and Shirk's part in it need checking.
* **The Marine National Bank at Erie.** The obituaries name him as its architect without qualification; Tatman says only that the prize-winning "building in Erie" was "probably" the bank.[1][2][5]
* **Dates of the European stay.** Tatman says he left in September 1888 for fourteen months, and returned to Philadelphia in 1891.[2] Fourteen months from September 1888 ends late in 1889; where he was in 1890 is not stated.
* **The partnership with Hutton.** The Institute's journal says that Hutton "was later admitted" to the partnership of Hillman and Shirk; Tatman says that in 1892 the partners "formally returned to Hutton as principals in the firm".[1][2]
* **Brother and sister.** David Marshall Shirk and Fannie Shirk are named only in a census index entry that has not been seen.[10]
* **"Washington" in 1915.** One tree gives him a residence "about 1915" at "Washington", citing a Knoxville newspaper report of his daughter's wedding that has not been seen.[11]
* **His daughter's year of birth** (1890 or 1893) and **his wife's parentage** (daughter or granddaughter of Chief Justice Watkins) are discussed in Anne Bell Burrage's biography.[8]
* **The American Institute of Architects' own directory entry** could not be read for this biography; the profile page cites it.[12]
