# Princess Vera Maximilianovna Kudasheva

🌳 [See in the GEDCOM Viewer](https://theo-armour.github.io/gedcom-viewer/#wiki=Vera-Maximilianovna-Kudasheva-von-Nieroth-1874-1920)

**Princess Vera Maximilianovna Kudasheva** (née **Countess von Nieroth**; Russian: Вера Максимилиановна Кудашева, урождённая фон Нирод; 23 July 1874 – 26 January 1920) was a Russian aristocrat and courtier who served as a Maid of Honor (*freilina*) to Empress Alexandra Feodorovna. Through her paternal Baltic-German family, the Counts von Nieroth, and her maternal Russian administrative family, the Trepovs, she belonged to the governing elite of the late Russian Empire. She is particularly noted in cultural history for her documented appearance at the 1903 Winter Palace Costume Ball, whose photographic record served as a visual source for the "Russian Style" (*Russkiy Stil*) playing card deck, and for her family's escape from Soviet Russia following the October Revolution of 1917.

## Early life and family background

Countess Vera Maximilianovna von Nieroth was born in Saint Petersburg on 23 July 1874 into two prominent families of the Russian Imperial service nobility.[1]

Her father, **Count Maximilian Carl Benedict von Nieroth** (1846–1914), belonged to the House of von Nieroth, a Baltic-German (*Ostsee*) noble family originally from Westphalia that had settled in Livonia and Estonia in the 15th century.[2] Ennobled as Swedish counts in 1706 under King Charles XII through Field Marshal Carl von Nieroth, the family was incorporated into the Russian nobility following the annexation of the Baltic provinces by Peter the Great and confirmed with the comital title in the Russian Empire.[2] Count Maximilian served at the Imperial Court in Saint Petersburg as a Chamberlain (*Kammerherr*), Master of the Hunt (*Jägermeister*), and member of the State Council.[1] Her brother, **Count Fyodor (Theodor) Maximilianovich von Nieroth** (1871–1952), graduated from the Page Corps, commanded the Life Guards Dragoon Regiment during World War I, and served in the White Volunteer Army during the Russian Civil War before emigrating to France.[2]

Her mother, **Anastasia Fyodorovna Trepova** (1849–1940), was a daughter of **General Fyodor Trepov** (1812–1889), who served as Governor of Saint Petersburg from 1866 to 1878 and survived an 1878 assassination attempt by the revolutionary Vera Zasulich.[3] Through her mother, Vera was the niece of three senior Imperial officials:
* **General Fyodor Trepov** (1854–1938), Governor-General of Kiev, Podolia, and Volhynia (1908–1914);[4]
* **General Dmitri Feodorovich Trepov** (1855–1906), Governor-General of Saint Petersburg and Palace Commandant during the Russian Revolution of 1905;[5]
* **Alexander Trepov** (1862–1928), Minister of Transport and Prime Minister of the Russian Empire from November 1916 to January 1917.[6]

## Court service and marriage

Prior to her marriage, Countess von Nieroth was appointed a Maid of Honor (*freilina*) to Empress Alexandra Feodorovna.[7] In 1894, she married **Prince Sergei Vladimirovich Kudashev** (1863–1933), a member of a Russian princely family of Tatar origin, an official in the Main Directorate of Appanages, an Imperial Chamberlain (*Kammerherr*), a corporate director, and a collector of 17th-century Dutch and Flemish Old Master paintings.[8][9]

The couple had two children:
* **Princess Maria (Myra) Sergeyevna Kudasheva** (1896–1990), who married the American diplomat Norman Armour in February 1919.[10]
* **Prince Sergei Sergeyevich Kudashev** (1901–1991).[1]

In Saint Petersburg, the family resided at Mokhovaya Street, 6 (documented in 1902)[8] and later at Bolshaya Morskaya Street, 57 (documented in city and court directories between 1915 and 1917).[11][12]

### 1903 Winter Palace Costume Ball

In February 1903, Princess Kudasheva attended the Costume Ball at the Winter Palace, held to commemorate the 290th anniversary of the Romanov dynasty, at which members of the court appeared in historically reconstructed 17th-century Muscovite dress.[13] Photographed in an embroidered *boyarina* costume and pearl *kokoshnik* headdress for the official commemorative album published in 1904, her portrait became one of the best-known visual records of the event.[13] Art historians have identified her costume photograph as one of the visual prototypes used by the Dondorf firm in designing the "Russian Style" (*Russkiy Stil*) playing card deck produced in 1911 for the Romanov tercentenary.[14]

## Revolutions of 1905 and 1917

During the Russian Revolution of 1905, Kudasheva's maternal relatives held central security posts in the capital, with her uncle Dmitri Feodorovich Trepov serving as Governor-General of Saint Petersburg and Palace Commandant until his death in September 1906.[5] During the same period, Prince Sergei Kudashev liquidated his art collection at auction in Amsterdam through Frederik Muller & Cie in June 1905 under the pseudonym "Prince Tatarsky," converting illiquid assets into capital abroad.[9]

In the winter of 1916–1917, her uncle Alexander Trepov briefly served as Prime Minister of Russia, clashing with Empress Alexandra over the political influence of Grigori Rasputin before being dismissed in January 1917, weeks prior to the February Revolution.[6]

---

## Exile and death

Following the October Revolution of 1917 and the Bolshevik nationalization of private banks and property in December 1917, the Kudashev family's court ranks and administrative connections placed them at risk of arrest.[10] With the assistance of **Norman Armour** (1887–1982), then Second Secretary at the United States Embassy in Petrograd—who obtained false travel documents for the family and was briefly detained by Bolshevik authorities for aiding their departure—Princess Vera, Prince Sergei, and their two children crossed the Finnish border during the winter of 1917–1918 and traveled to neutral Sweden.[10]

In Sweden, the family resided at **Villa Skärtofta** in Saltsjöbaden near Stockholm, where their home became a meeting point for exiled Russian aristocrats and diplomats.[10] Following the marriage of her daughter Maria to Norman Armour in Brussels in February 1919, Princess Vera relocated to France, where her widowed mother Anastasia Fyodorovna and brother Count Fyodor von Nieroth had also settled in exile.[1][2]

Princess Vera Maximilianovna Kudasheva died in Paris on 26 January 1920 at the age of 45.[1][15]

---

## Historiographical note

Some 20th- and 21st-century bibliographic databases have erroneously attributed to Princess Vera Maximilianovna Kudasheva a 32-page Moscow pamphlet published in 1901 under the name "Vera Kudasheva," titled *From a Russian Woman: A Reply to N. A. Lukhmanova Regarding Her Book "The Cause of the Eternal Strife Between Man and Woman"* (Russian: *От русской женщины: Ответ Н. А. Лухмановой по поводу её книги «Причина вечной распри между мужчиной и женщиной»*).[16] Philological research has established that the 1901 pamphlet was written by an unrelated namesake of peasant origin who worked as a rural schoolteacher.[17]
