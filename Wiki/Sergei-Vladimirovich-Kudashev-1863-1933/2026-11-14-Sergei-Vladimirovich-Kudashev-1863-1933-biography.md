# Prince Sergei Vladimirovitch Kudashev (1863–1933)

**Prince Sergei Vladimirovitch Kudashev** (*Князь Сергей Владимирович Кудашев*; December 14 [O.S. September 14], 1863 – February 27/28, 1933) was a Russian nobleman, Imperial courtier, corporate financier, and art connoisseur who navigated the final decades of the Romanov Empire before escaping the 1917 Revolution into European exile.

---

* **Full Name:** Prince Sergei Vladimirovitch Kudashev (*Князь Сергей Владимирович Кудашев*)

  * **Florence, Italy:** Villa Granduchessa (c. 1920–1933)

## I. Aristocratic Lineage and Early Life

Born in Kiev in 1863, Prince Sergei Vladimirovitch Kudashev belonged to an ancient princely dynasty of Tatar origin whose noble title had been recognized in Russia since the 17th century. The southern branch of the family maintained its ancestral landed seat in **Chigirin Uyezd** of Kiev Governorate (modern-day Cherkasy Oblast, Ukraine), centered on the estate of **Veselyi Kut**—acquired in 1804 by his great-grandfather, Prince Daniil Ivanovich Kudashev—and the nearby settlement of **Kudashevo**. His great-uncle, Major General Prince Nikolai Danilovich Kudashev (1784–1813), married Field Marshal Mikhail Kutuzov's daughter and won fame as a partisan commander during the 1812 Patriotic War.

Sergei was the son of **Prince Vladimir Sergeyevich Kudashev** (1833–1871) and **Princess Olga Nikolaevna Horvath** (1840–1907). When his father died in 1871, eight-year-old Sergei inherited an initial cabinet of paintings, rare books, and numismatics. Under the guidance of his family—including his paternal uncle, Moscow banking official and historian Prince Sergei Sergeyevich Kudashev (1834–1915)—young Sergei developed the scholarly tastes in European art and history that would define his adulthood.

---

## II. Imperial Court Service and Corporate Career

Entering state service in St. Petersburg during the reign of Emperor Nicholas II, Kudashev joined the **Main Directorate of Appanages** (*Главное управление уделов*), the Imperial ministry responsible for administering the private estates, agricultural lands, and revenues of the Romanov family. His court advancement reflected his close standing in St. Petersburg high society:
* **1899:** Appointed *Kammerjunker* (Gentleman of the Bedchamber) of the Imperial Court.
* **1908:** Elevated to *Kammerherr* (Chamberlain) of the Imperial Court, holding the civil rank of Active State Councillor.

In 1894, Kudashev married **Countess Vera Maximilianovna von Nieroth** (1874–1920), a former maid of honor (*фрейлина*) to Empress Alexandra Feodorovna. The couple stood at the center of Imperial court life, famously photographed in traditional 17th-century Muscovite boyar attire at the celebrated **1903 Winter Palace Costume Ball**.

Alongside his court duties, Kudashev transitioned into one of St. Petersburg's prominent corporate directors during the pre-war industrial boom. Living first at **Mokhovaya Ulitsa, 6** and later at **Bol'shaya Morskaya Ulitsa, 57**, he held seats on the boards of directors of several major enterprises:
* **Russian Trade and Industrial Bank** (*Русский торгово-промышленный банк*)
* **Insurance Company "Salamandra"** (*Страховое товарищество «Саламандра»*)
* **Trade and Industrial Company "Alfa-Nobel"** (*Торгово-промышленное товарищество «Альфа-Нобель»*)
* **Podolia Railway Company** (*Общество Подольской железной дороги*)

## III. The Old Master Art Collection (1902–1905)

Expanding upon the cabinet left by his father, Kudashev assembled a museum-grade gallery specializing in **17th-century Dutch and Flemish Old Masters**, alongside early printed books and historical medals. His gallery featured notable works by **Pieter Brueghel the Younger** (*The Peasant Wedding*), **Jan van Goyen** (*A Sandy Road with Thatched Cottage*), **Cornelis van Poelenburgh**, **Jan van Noordt**, and **Theodoor van Thulden**.

Treating his collection as a serious scholarly pursuit, Kudashev invited leading European and Russian art historians—including Dutch authority **Cornelis Hofstede de Groot** and Russian critic **Alexandre Benois**—to study and attribute his paintings. In **1902**, he privately published an illustrated folio catalogue in St. Petersburg bound with his gilt princely coat of arms:
* *Vingt-cinq tableaux de la collection du prince Serge Wladimirowich Koudacheff à St. Pétersbourg (Mokhowaja 6)* (St. Petersburg: A. I. Vil'borg, 1902).

Amid the political and financial turmoil of the 1905 Russian Revolution and the Russo-Japanese War, Kudashev made the consequential decision to liquidate his painting gallery abroad. On **June 27, 1905**, his collection was auctioned in Amsterdam by **Frederik Muller & Cie** under the discreet aristocratic pseudonym **"Prince Tatarsky"** (*Catalogue des tableaux anciens formant la collection du Prince Tatarsky à St. Pétersbourg*). Many works entered major European collections—including purchases by Swedish collector Wilhelmina von Hallwyl now housed in Stockholm's Hallwyl Museum—while preserving liquid capital for Kudashev over a decade before the Bolshevik nationalizations of 1917–1918.

---

## IV. The 1917 Revolution and Escape to Sweden

Following the collapse of the Russian monarchy in 1917 and the October Bolshevik seizure of power, Kudashev's Imperial court titles and banking directorships placed him and his family in immediate peril. Private banks were nationalized in December 1917, and noble estates across Ukraine and Russia were expropriated.

With the assistance of **Norman Armour**—then Second Secretary at the American Embassy in Petrograd, who procured false identity documents for the family and was briefly imprisoned by Bolshevik authorities for aiding their flight—Prince Sergei, Princess Vera, and their two children, **Princess Maria (Myra)** (1896–1990) and **Prince Sergei** (1901–1991), escaped across the frozen Finnish border during the winter and made their way to neutral Sweden.

In Sweden, the family settled at **Villa Skärtofta** in Saltsjöbaden, outside Stockholm, where their residence became a prominent gathering point for exiled Russian aristocrats and diplomats. In February 1919, their daughter Maria married Norman Armour in Brussels, uniting the exiled Russian princely line with an American diplomatic career.

---

## V. Final Years in Florence (1920–1933)

Following the death of Princess Vera in 1920, Kudashev relocated south to **Florence, Italy**, a city long favored by Russian art connoisseurs that hosted a close-knit White Russian colony centered around the Russian Orthodox Church of the Nativity of Christ and St. Nicholas. Having sold his Old Master gallery in 1905 rather than losing it to confiscation, Kudashev was able to maintain a comfortable aristocratic residence at **Villa Granduchessa**.

In Florence, Kudashev formed a second household with **Olga Erikovna von Pistohlkors** (1888–1963), a fellow Baltic-German/Russian aristocrat. In **1926**, their daughter **Olga Sergeyevna Kudasheva** (later **Olga Pelà**, who married Italian historian and translator Achille Pelà) was born.

Prince Sergei Vladimirovitch Kudashev died in Florence on **February 27/28, 1933**, at the age of 69. He was laid to rest in Florence's historic non-Catholic **Cimitero Evangelico agli Allori** (Via Senese, 184), Plot Q. III.017.
